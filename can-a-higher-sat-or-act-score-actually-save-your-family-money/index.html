Can a Higher SAT or ACT Score Actually Save Your Family Money?

September 24, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Table of Contents

  2. Why This Changes the ROI Calculation for Test Prep
  3. Test Scores and Scholarships: What Families Often Miss
  4. The Pathfinders Approach: Test Strategy as Part of the Whole Plan

Many families think about SAT and ACT scores primarily in terms of college admission. A strong score helps a student get in. A weak score might hurt the application. That framing makes sense as far as it goes – but it misses one of the most financially significant uses of standardized test scores: merit scholarships.

At Pathfinders College & Career Advisors, we help families think about the college decision as a financial investment, not just an academic milestone. And one of the clearest examples of how a test score can translate directly into dollars is the relationship between ACT and SAT scores and institutional merit aid.

Merit Scholarships Are Real Money – and They’re Often Score-Driven

Many colleges and universities offer automatic merit scholarships based on a combination of a student’s GPA and standardized test score. These scholarships don’t require a separate application. They don’t require demonstrated financial need. They’re offered to students who meet specific academic criteria – and the scholarship amount often increases significantly as the test score increases.

To make this concrete, here’s an example drawn from the University of Mississippi’s published 2026-2027 nonresident freshman automatic merit scholarship schedule. This is a real institution with a public, published scholarship grid:

For students with a GPA of 3.50-3.74:

  • ACT 28-29: $10,000 per year
  • ACT 30-31: $15,000 per year

For students with a GPA of 3.0-3.49:

  • ACT 26-27: $5,000 per year
  • ACT 28-29: $8,000 per year
  • ACT 30-31: $10,000 per year
  • ACT 32+: $15,000 per year

In practical terms: a student with a 3.2 GPA who improves their ACT from a 27 to a 30 could increase their annual scholarship by $5,000. Over four years, that’s a $20,000 difference – for improving a single test score by three points.

That’s not a hypothetical. That’s the published scholarship grid of one real university. And many institutions operate similarly.

Why This Changes the ROI Calculation for Test Prep

Families sometimes hesitate to invest in test preparation because of the cost. A solid test prep course or tutoring program can run anywhere from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. That’s not a trivial amount.

But consider the math. If a student’s baseline ACT score is a 27 and a targeted preparation program helps them reach a 30, and that improvement qualifies them for an additional $5,000 per year in merit aid at a school that’s a good fit – that’s $20,000 over four years in return for a test prep investment of perhaps $1,000 to $2,000. The return on investment is substantial.

Even a more modest improvement – say, from a 27 to a 28 – can matter at institutions with score-based scholarship tiers. The key is knowing which schools on the student’s list use score-based merit aid, what the specific thresholds are, and where the student’s current score sits relative to those thresholds.

Test Scores and Scholarships: What Families Often Miss

The conversation families most often have about test scores focuses on admission. But the scholarship question is just as important – and sometimes more important financially. Here’s what families should understand:

Admission and scholarship eligibility are separate questions

A student may be admitted to a college at one test score level but qualify for a significantly larger scholarship with a higher score. At test-optional schools, a student who chooses not to submit a score might be admitted – but might also be leaving scholarship money on the table if a competitive score would have qualified them for merit aid.

Scholarship thresholds vary by institution

Every college that offers score-based merit aid sets its own thresholds. There’s no universal chart that applies everywhere. Families need to research the specific merit aid policies of each school on the student’s list – and do that research early, before deciding how much to invest in test preparation.

The “right” school is the one that best fits the whole picture

A student might be admitted to a highly selective school with no scholarship and also be offered a substantial merit scholarship at a school that’s a strong fit academically and a better value financially. Standardized test scores can be one of the levers that opens up better-fit options – not just higher-prestige options.

The Pathfinders Approach: Test Strategy as Part of the Whole Plan

We don’t view test preparation in isolation. We view it as one element of a broader strategy that starts with career direction, moves through educational fit, and always keeps the financial picture in focus.

That means helping families understand which schools on the list use score-based merit aid and at what thresholds, where the student’s current score sits relative to those thresholds, whether targeted test preparation is likely to produce a meaningful return, and how test scores interact with GPA and other academic credentials in the scholarship calculation.

Pathfinders partners with College Tutors, which offers free diagnostic tests for both the SAT and ACT. This is a smart starting point – it gives families a realistic baseline score before committing to a full preparation program, and it helps identify which test is likely the better fit for that student.

A Note on Research and Verification

Scholarship programs, eligibility criteria, and score thresholds change. What a school offers for the 2026-2027 academic year may differ in future years. Families should always verify current scholarship policies directly with each institution before making decisions about test preparation investment or school selection.

The broader principle, though, remains consistent: for students who are in a position to improve their test score, the financial return on that investment can be significant – and families who factor scholarships into the testing decision are often better positioned than those who focus on admission alone.
