What to Do If You’re Waitlisted: A Step-by-Step Guide for Parents

August 4, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Table of Contents

  1. First: Understand What the Waitlist Actually Means
  2. Step-by-Step: What to Do Right Now
  3. What NOT to Do
  4. A Note on Timing
  5. Final Thought

The waitlist notification arrives, and the room goes quiet. Your student opened their admissions portal expecting a clear answer – accepted or denied – and instead received something that feels like neither.

A waitlist placement is genuinely hard to navigate. It is not a rejection, but it is not an acceptance either. It leaves families in a holding pattern at exactly the moment when they were hoping for clarity.

The good news: there is a lot a family can do. How you respond to a waitlist placement – in the first 48 hours and in the weeks that follow – can meaningfully affect the outcome. This guide walks you through every step in order.

First: Understand What the Waitlist Actually Means

A waitlist is a reserve list that colleges use to manage enrollment. After the May 1st enrollment deposit deadline, schools look at how many students are confirmed – and if the incoming class is smaller than projected, they turn to the waitlist to fill the remaining spots.

Some important context: nationally, colleges admit roughly 20% of students who accept a spot on the waitlist. At the most selective institutions, that number can drop to 7% or lower. Waitlist outcomes vary enormously by year. The majority of waitlist decisions are made in May, typically in the two weeks after May 1st.

Knowing these numbers is not meant to discourage – it is meant to reset expectations. The goal is for your student to have a genuine Plan A backup while still pursuing the waitlisted school with intention.

Step-by-Step: What to Do Right Now

Step 1: Read the Waitlist Notification Carefully

Every school handles the waitlist differently. Some allow a letter of continued interest; others explicitly ask for nothing additional. Read the notification and any linked instructions carefully before doing anything else. Acting outside the school’s stated process can create a poor impression at the worst possible moment.

Step 2: Formally Accept the Waitlist Spot – Quickly

Most colleges require students to actively opt in to remain on the waitlist, typically through their admissions portal. This step is often time-sensitive – missing the deadline means forfeiting the spot. If your student wants to remain in consideration, complete this step within 24 to 48 hours.

Step 3: Secure Your Backup School by May 1st

This step is non-negotiable. Your student must submit an enrollment deposit to a school they genuinely plan to attend by the May 1st National Candidates’ Reply Date – regardless of their waitlist status. This deposit is almost always non-refundable.

Choosing a backup school is not giving up on the waitlisted school. It is responsible planning. Submitting a deposit to a backup school while remaining on a waitlist is completely standard and expected.

Step 4: Write a Letter of Continued Interest (LOCI)

If the school’s instructions allow additional communication – and most do – the Letter of Continued Interest is your student’s most powerful tool. A LOCI is a brief, focused letter (roughly 400 to 500 words) sent directly to the admissions office.

Done well, it accomplishes three things: confirms unambiguously that if admitted, your student will attend (demonstrated interest matters significantly at most schools); updates the admissions office on any new accomplishments since the original application; and reinforces specifically why this school is the right fit.

What makes a strong LOCI: It is personal and specific – referencing particular professors, programs, or courses. It includes a clear statement: “If admitted, I will attend.” It is concise – one page maximum. And it is professionally written and free of errors.

Step 5: Submit Any New Updates or Achievements

If your student has something genuinely new and significant to share – a major award, a notable improvement in grades, a significant project, or a new leadership role – submit it. This can be included in the LOCI or submitted separately, depending on the school’s guidance.

Step 6: Maintain Strong Grades Through the End of the Year

If a college pulls from its waitlist in May, one of the first things they may request is the student’s most recent transcript. A significant drop in senior year grades can disqualify a waitlisted student even if their overall application was strong. Most waitlist offers include a condition that final grades meet a certain standard.

Step 7: Wait Thoughtfully – and Take Care of Your Student

After completing steps one through six, most of the waiting is out of your hands. The best parent response combines empathy with structure. Acknowledge the difficulty of waiting. Let your student grieve the disappointment if they need to. But also help them stay engaged with the backup school – attending orientation events, joining admitted student groups, and connecting with future classmates.

What NOT to Do

Do not call the admissions office repeatedly – the vast majority of schools will not have waitlist information until the second week of May at the earliest. Do not have parents, alumni contacts, or donors contact the admissions office on the student’s behalf. Do not send unsolicited materials beyond what the school explicitly allows. Do not declare first-choice interest to multiple schools simultaneously – admissions offices communicate with each other more than families expect.

A Note on Timing

Waitlist movement happens almost entirely in the three weeks between May 1st and late May. Schools assess their yield against enrollment targets in the days after May 1st, then waitlist offers go out on a rolling basis as spots open. Some schools resolve their waitlists entirely by June 1st; others carry waitlisted students into the summer.

Final Thought

A waitlist placement is not the end of the road – but it does require a deliberate response. Families who handle it well focus on what they can control: the LOCI, the updates, the grades, and the commitment to a genuine backup plan.
