ACT to SAT Score Conversion Chart: Which Score Is Better for College Admissions?

August 11, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

You took the ACT. Your friend took the SAT. Now you’re both applying to the same colleges and wondering: whose score is actually better?

This is one of the most common – and most consequential – questions students ask during the application process. Because how you answer it affects which test you invest time preparing for, whether you retake, and how you present your academic profile to admissions offices.

This guide gives you the official ACT-to-SAT concordance chart and, more importantly, the context to use it strategically.

How the Two Tests Are Structured (and Why It Matters)

The ACT and SAT test similar academic skills through different structures:

  • The ACT scores on a scale of 1-36, averaged across four sections: English, Mathematics, Reading, and Science.
  • The SAT (redesigned) scores on a scale of 400-1600 across two sections: Evidence-Based Reading and Writing (EBRW) and Math.

The ACT is generally considered to test speed more directly – you have less time per question. The SAT historically rewards careful reading and reasoning, though the gap has narrowed with the digital SAT. Neither test is inherently “harder.” What matters is which format plays to your individual strengths.

Do Colleges Prefer One Test Over the Other?

The short answer: no. Every accredited four-year college in the United States accepts both the ACT and the SAT. There is no admissions advantage built into preferring one test.

Admissions officers are trained to read both scores using concordance charts similar to the one above. A 32 on the ACT and a 1430 on the SAT land in essentially the same competitive range – neither gives you an inherent edge over the other in the application review.

What matters is presenting your strongest score. And your strongest score comes from the test format that suits how you think, read, and work under timed pressure.

How to Know Which Test Is Right for You

Take a full-length practice test of each under real timed conditions. Compare the results using the concordance chart above. The test where your performance is relatively stronger – even before preparation – is almost always the better investment of your study time.

You may do better on the ACT if:

  • You’re strong in science and data interpretation.
  • You work quickly and don’t overthink questions.
  • You prefer straightforward question formats with less ambiguity.

You may do better on the SAT if:

  • You’re a strong analytical reader and process complex text well.
  • You’re comfortable with a digital testing format (the new SAT is fully adaptive and computer-based).
  • You prefer a two-section structure vs. the ACT’s four.

The Score Submission Question: Superscore and Test Optional

Two additional factors shape your test strategy:

Superscoring: Many schools now superscore the ACT – meaning they take the highest section scores across multiple test dates and calculate a composite. Others superscore only the SAT, or both. Before deciding how many times to test, check whether your target schools superscore each test. Superscoring can meaningfully boost your competitive composite.

Test-Optional Policies: Many schools extended test-optional policies through the mid-2020s, though a significant number of selective schools – including MIT, Yale, and Dartmouth – have returned to test requirements. Always check each school’s current policy. When test-optional is available, submit scores only if they strengthen your application. Submitting a weak score at a test-optional school is never required and can hurt more than help.

The Pathfinders Perspective: Test Scores Are a Tool, Not a Destination

Test scores are a threshold, not a destination. They get you past the first screen at selective schools – they don’t determine whether the school is the right fit for your student’s career direction and future.

Families often spend enormous time and money on test preparation, retakes, and coaching. That investment can absolutely be worthwhile – but only within a broader context of knowing where the student is headed, which schools fit that direction, and what score range is meaningful at those specific institutions.

Scoring a 34 on the ACT for a school where the median admitted student has a 28 is over-preparation. Scoring a 26 and applying to schools where the 25th percentile is a 31 wastes application fees and emotional energy. The strategic move is to build your college list based on career fit first – then identify what test score range makes you a competitive applicant at those specific schools.

When parents and students approach testing this way, the process becomes considerably less stressful – because “good enough” has a clear definition. Pathfinders Advisors can help your family build that framework through a complimentary strategy call.

Three Things to Do with This Information Today

  1. Take a practice test for both the ACT and SAT. Your natural performance difference is the most reliable signal for which test to prioritize.
  2. Look up the 25th-75th percentile test score range at each school on your college list. That range tells you where you need to be – not a generic “good score” number.
  3. Build the college list based on career fit first. Then use test scores to calibrate where you’re a strong applicant – rather than choosing schools based on where your score gets you in.

Final Thoughts

The ACT and SAT are both valid, widely accepted measures of academic readiness. Use the concordance chart to compare scores accurately, choose the test that showcases your genuine strengths, and submit the score that represents your strongest performance at schools where it adds value.

But remember: the score gets you to the table. What keeps you there – and what leads to a successful, purposeful college experience – is clarity about where you’re headed and why.
