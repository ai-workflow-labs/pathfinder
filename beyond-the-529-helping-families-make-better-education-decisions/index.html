Beyond the 529: Helping Families Make Better Education Decisions

July 10, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

For many families, funding a child’s education begins with a 529 plan. Parents contribute consistently for years, grandparents make generous gifts, and advisors help clients build tax-efficient education savings strategies. But once it’s time to use those funds, many families discover something surprising: They’ve built a savings plan — but not a decision-making plan.

Saving for college is important. Spending those dollars wisely is where lasting financial value is created. As financial advisors, helping clients maximize the return on their education investment can become just as valuable as helping them accumulate the assets in the first place. Understanding the hidden cost of college without a plan is often the first step toward better decision-making.

A 529 Plan Is a Funding Tool—Not a Decision Tool

A 529 plan answers one important question: “How will we pay for education?” It doesn’t answer equally important questions like: Which educational path offers the greatest return? Is a four-year university the right first step? Does the student’s career goal actually require that degree? How much debt is appropriate? What happens if the student changes majors?

Without those conversations, families often make six-figure decisions based on campus tours, rankings, or emotion rather than long-term outcomes. That’s where advisors can create tremendous value. A solid college financial guide for parents can help frame these critical conversations before any enrollment decisions are made.

Start With Career Exploration—Not College Selection

Many students begin researching colleges before they have a clear understanding of themselves. They know where they want to go long before they know why they’re going. Career exploration should come first. When students understand their interests, strengths, values, and preferred work environments, choosing an educational pathway becomes much more intentional.

Instead of asking: “Which college should I attend?” the better question is: “What education will best prepare me for the career and life I want?” That shift often leads to smarter decisions about majors, schools, internships, certifications, and even alternative education pathways.

ROE™ Matters More Than Prestige

Families naturally gravitate toward recognizable university names. Prestige feels like security. But brand recognition doesn’t always produce the strongest financial outcome. A student graduating from a public university with manageable debt and a clear career direction may achieve financial independence years sooner than a graduate carrying significant debt from a more expensive institution.

The goal isn’t finding the “best” college — it’s finding the best value for that individual student. When evaluating education decisions, encourage families to consider: total cost of attendance, expected debt at graduation, graduation rates, career placement outcomes, salary potential within the chosen field, and opportunities for internships and experiential learning.

The Real Cost of Changing Majors

Changing majors is common. It’s also expensive. Students who change majors frequently add semesters, delay graduation, accumulate additional tuition costs, and may require more student loans. Those extra semesters also represent opportunity cost — graduating one year later means entering the workforce one year later, delaying earnings, retirement savings, and wealth accumulation.

Helping students gain greater career clarity before selecting a major can significantly reduce these unnecessary costs. Understanding how to budget and navigate the FAFSA alongside career planning creates a more complete picture for families making these decisions.

The Hidden Cost of Poor Educational Fit

One of the largest education expenses rarely appears on a tuition bill: poor fit. When students enroll at schools that don’t align with their academic interests, learning style, social preferences, or career goals, the consequences can include transferring schools, changing majors multiple times, reduced academic performance, lower engagement, increased stress, delayed graduation, or dropping out altogether. These outcomes create both emotional and financial strain for families.

Align Education Spending With Long-Term Financial Goals

Every dollar spent on education is a dollar that isn’t available for other priorities. Families often balance competing goals: retirement savings, supporting younger siblings, purchasing a home, caring for aging parents, building emergency reserves, or launching a business. Helping clients evaluate education spending within the context of their entire financial plan leads to better long-term decisions.

Sometimes that means choosing an in-state university. Sometimes it means starting at a community college before transferring. Sometimes it means selecting a private institution because the financial aid package makes it the better value. To explore how financial aid appeals can meaningfully change the cost equation, advisors and families should engage that conversation early.

Helping Families Think Beyond the Savings Account

Today’s families don’t need more information about college. They need better guidance. Financial advisors are uniquely positioned to broaden the conversation from “How do we pay for college?” to “How do we make the smartest education investment possible?” That discussion strengthens advisor-client relationships while helping families make one of the largest financial decisions of their lives with greater confidence.
