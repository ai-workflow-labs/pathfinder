Does the PSAT Still Matter in 2026?

October 13, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Every fall, parents of high school students hear some version of the same announcement: “Your student will be taking the PSAT.” And many parents have the same questions.

Does the PSAT actually matter? Should my freshman take it? What about my sophomore? Does a college ever see the score? Can it lead to scholarship money? And with so many colleges continuing to offer test-optional admissions, is preparing for the PSAT even worth the effort?

The short answer is: Yes, the PSAT can still be valuable in 2026, but its value depends heavily on what grade your student is in and what you do with the results. At Pathfinders, we don’t believe students should take tests simply because everyone else is taking them. The question should always be: How can we use this information to make better decisions?

First, There Isn’t Just One PSAT

The College Board offers three PSAT-related assessments:

  • PSAT 8/9 – Designed for students in grades 8 and 9.
  • PSAT 10 – Designed for sophomores and administered in the spring.
  • PSAT/NMSQT – Typically taken by sophomores and juniors in the fall.

The PSAT 10 and PSAT/NMSQT are essentially the same test. The important difference is that the PSAT/NMSQT is the qualifying examination for the National Merit Scholarship Program. Scores from the PSAT 10 and PSAT 8/9 do not qualify students for National Merit. That distinction becomes particularly important during junior year.

Grade 9: Establish a Baseline, Don’t Create Pressure

For most freshmen, we view the PSAT as a diagnostic tool, not a high-stakes event. Some schools offer the PSAT 8/9, which is specifically designed for eighth and ninth graders and measures the same general skills that will eventually appear on the SAT. If your freshman’s school offers it, taking the test can be worthwhile. But we generally would not recommend spending months preparing for it.

Instead, use the results to answer questions such as: Where is my student naturally strong? Is math significantly stronger than reading and writing? Are there foundational skills that need attention? How does the student handle a timed digital standardized test?

The goal in ninth grade isn’t to produce an impressive score. The goal is to gather information. That information gives families time to address academic weaknesses before junior year, when standardized testing can begin to affect college strategy.

Grade 10: This Is Where the PSAT Becomes More Useful

Sophomore year is when we start paying closer attention. Students may take either the PSAT 10 in the spring or the PSAT/NMSQT in the fall, depending on what their school offers. For a sophomore, the test serves several purposes.

First, it provides a much better indication of where the student might eventually perform on the SAT. Second, it identifies specific academic areas that may need improvement. Third, it gives students experience with the actual digital testing environment they will encounter later. Fourth, it can help families begin making an important strategic decision: Should this student invest time preparing for the SAT?

A student performing very well as a sophomore may have significant upside from targeted preparation. A student whose scores are less competitive may need additional academic development before formal test preparation begins. And for some students, the results may ultimately support a different testing strategy. The sophomore PSAT gives families time to make that decision thoughtfully rather than waiting until junior spring.

Grade 11: Now the PSAT Can Matter Financially

Junior year is different. For most students following a traditional four-year high school path, the junior-year PSAT/NMSQT is the test that determines eligibility for the National Merit Scholarship Program. That means strong students should not approach the junior PSAT as simply another practice test.

National Merit recognition can create opportunities for scholarships and, importantly, can make a student particularly attractive to colleges actively recruiting high-achieving students. This connects directly to one of our favorite questions at Pathfinders: Where is your student wanted? A very strong PSAT score may help answer that question. Some colleges provide significant merit packages to students with strong academic profiles and National Merit recognition. For the right student, that can turn an October test into part of a much larger college affordability strategy.

What Does the PSAT Look Like in 2026?

The PSAT is now fully digital and administered through the College Board’s Bluebook application. The PSAT/NMSQT takes approximately 2 hours and 14 minutes and contains two sections:

There is no essay. The test is also adaptive – each section contains two modules. A student’s performance on the first module influences the difficulty of questions presented in the second module. Students can move backward and forward within a module, but once a module ends, they cannot return to it. There is also a calculator built directly into Bluebook for the Math section, although students may bring an approved calculator if they prefer.

How Is the PSAT Scored?

The PSAT/NMSQT and PSAT 10 use a total score range of 320 to 1520. Reading and Writing ranges from 160 to 760. Math ranges from 160 to 760. Students also receive percentile information that helps families understand how their performance compares with other students.

But don’t become overly focused on the total score. We are often more interested in the story behind the score. A 1250 with very strong math and significantly weaker reading tells us something. A balanced 1250 tells us something different. A student whose score jumps substantially from freshman to sophomore year gives us another useful piece of information. The trend can matter as much as the number.

What About National Merit?

The PSAT/NMSQT generates a separate National Merit Selection Index, which ranges from 48 to 228. National Merit recognition is highly competitive, and qualifying thresholds can vary. For students who are already demonstrating very strong academic performance, sophomore PSAT results can help determine whether pursuing National Merit recognition during junior year is realistic.

That’s one situation where we may recommend intentional PSAT preparation. If a sophomore is already performing near National Merit territory, preparing specifically for the junior PSAT could potentially have a financial return. For many other students, extensive PSAT-specific preparation isn’t necessary. Instead, SAT preparation can accomplish both objectives because the exams measure closely related skills.

Should Students Prepare for the PSAT?

Our answer depends on the grade:

  • Freshman: Minimal preparation. Understand the digital format and get a good night’s sleep. Treat the test as a baseline.
  • Sophomore: Some preparation can be useful. Students should become familiar with Bluebook and ideally complete an official practice test. Beginning in fall 2026, College Board is providing new full-length PSAT practice tests through Bluebook. The objective is primarily diagnostic.
  • Junior: Preparation may absolutely make sense – particularly for students who scored very highly as sophomores, may be competitive for National Merit, plan to take the SAT, or are targeting colleges where strong scores could strengthen admission or merit-aid opportunities. For those students, the PSAT isn’t simply practice. It can be part of the admissions and financial strategy.

But What About Test-Optional Colleges?

This is where families sometimes make a mistake. Test optional does not necessarily mean tests have no value. The better question is: Will a strong score improve this student’s position? A strong SAT score may potentially strengthen an application at some colleges. It may support merit scholarship consideration at some institutions. It may confirm academic strength. And it may give a student another positive piece of information to include in an application.

The decision should be student-specific and college-specific. At Pathfinders, we don’t believe every student needs to submit a test score. We also don’t believe students should automatically walk away from testing simply because a college is test optional. Optional means you have a choice. The goal is to determine which choice puts the student in the strongest position.

What Parents Should Do When PSAT Scores Arrive

Don’t simply look at the number and close the report. Use the results to start a conversation. Ask:

  1. Where are the student’s strongest academic areas?
  2. Where are the largest skill gaps?
  3. How does this score compare with the student’s college goals?
  4. Is National Merit potentially realistic?
  5. Would SAT preparation likely produce a meaningful return?
  6. Could a strong future SAT score improve admission or merit scholarship opportunities?

Those questions turn the PSAT from a test into a planning tool.

The Bigger Opportunity

The PSAT should never become another source of unnecessary pressure. For most students, particularly in ninth and tenth grade, it is simply information. But information becomes valuable when families know what to do with it.

A PSAT score can help identify academic strengths. It can reveal weaknesses while there is still time to address them. It can help determine whether SAT preparation is worthwhile. It can identify potential National Merit candidates. And eventually, it can become one piece of the larger question we want every family to ask: Where is my student wanted?

Because the goal isn’t simply to produce the highest test score possible. The goal is to use every available piece of information to help students identify colleges where they are academically competitive, financially attractive and positioned to pursue the career they ultimately want. That’s the difference between taking a test because everyone else takes it and using the test strategically.
