Return on Education™ (ROE™) by Major: The College Ranking Families Should Be Paying Attention To

September 23, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Table of Contents

  2. Major Matters… a Lot
  3. Where You Earn the Degree Can Matter Too
  4. This Changes the College ROI Conversation

For decades, families have asked a familiar question: “What’s the best college my child can get into?”

But there may be a much more financially important question: “What’s the return on the degree my child plans to earn there?”

That question just became significantly more visible as U.S. News & World Report made a major change to its 2027 Best Colleges rankings by introducing a new measure called Earnings by Major. The change was significant enough to help end Princeton University’s 15-year run as the nation’s No. 1-ranked university. MIT is now No. 1.

Why? In part, because U.S. News has begun measuring something families increasingly care about: what will my child earn from this major after graduation.

What Is “Earnings by Major”?

The methodology is more sophisticated than simply asking which university has graduates with the highest salaries. U.S. News uses U.S. Department of Education College Scorecard data and examines earnings four years after graduation for employed graduates who received federal financial aid and whose highest degree is a bachelor’s degree.

But here’s the important part: U.S. News compares students within their field of study. An engineering graduate isn’t simply compared with an English graduate. Engineering graduates are compared with other engineering graduates. Business graduates are compared with other business graduates. Computer science graduates are compared with other computer science graduates.

That attempts to answer a much more useful question: How well do graduates of this particular university perform financially compared with people who studied the same subject elsewhere?

MIT ranked #1 on the new Earnings by Major factor, followed by Caltech at #2 and Carnegie Mellon at #3. That contributed to some significant movement in the overall rankings. Carnegie Mellon, for example, jumped six places to #14 overall, while Caltech climbed six positions to tie for #5.

This doesn’t mean those universities suddenly became better schools. It means the definition of college value is beginning to change.

Major Matters… a Lot

Before we even consider the university, families need to understand how dramatically earnings can differ by field of study. Recent College Scorecard-based national data illustrate the difference in median earnings roughly four years after graduation:

These aren’t starting salaries, nor are they guarantees of what an individual student will earn. They are broad national outcomes, and career earnings can vary tremendously by occupation, geography, graduate education, employer and individual performance.

But look at the spread. Computer science graduates are earning around $92,000 at this measurement point, while teaching graduates are around $52,000 and visual and performing arts graduates around $42,000. That doesn’t mean a student shouldn’t study education or the arts. It means the amount a family should consider investing in those educational pathways deserves a thoughtful discussion and a process.

Where You Earn the Degree Can Matter Too

This is where the new U.S. News methodology becomes particularly interesting. Consider two students who both major in computer science. One graduates from an institution where employers aggressively recruit graduates into technology, artificial intelligence and quantitative finance. Another earns the same major from a university with fewer recruiting relationships and weaker placement outcomes.

The diploma may say Computer Science in both cases. The economic outcomes can be very different.

U.S. News specifically cited this phenomenon when explaining the new rankings. Highly technical institutions such as MIT, Caltech and Carnegie Mellon performed particularly well even after graduates were compared with people studying the same fields elsewhere.

In other words: The major matters. But the ecosystem surrounding that major may matter too – including internships, employer relationships, alumni networks, geographic location, experiential learning and career placement.

This Changes the College ROI Conversation

Imagine two universities:

College A: Four-year net family investment of $320,000. Expected earnings four years after graduation in the student’s intended field: $75,000.

College B: Four-year net family investment of $180,000. Expected earnings for graduates in the same field: $82,000.

Which is the better educational investment? There may be solid reasons to choose College A – perhaps it provides a better academic environment, perhaps the student is dramatically more likely to graduate there, perhaps its alumni network creates opportunities that aren’t reflected in four-year earnings data. But now we’re asking the right question.

Not: “Which college ranks higher?” But: “What are we receiving for the additional $140,000 investment?” That is a financial planning conversation.

Don’t Confuse Earnings With Value

There is an important caution. Salary cannot become the only measure of education. Teachers matter. Social workers matter. Artists matter. Researchers matter. And students who choose lower-paying careers can have tremendously successful and fulfilling lives.

A student also shouldn’t choose engineering simply because engineers earn more if they have no aptitude or interest in engineering. That’s why we believe the conversation should begin even earlier: Career first. College second.

Understand the student’s interests, strengths, aptitudes and potential career directions. Then determine what educational pathway is actually required to pursue those paths. Then evaluate which institutions provide the strongest combination of career fit, academic fit, college fit and financial fit. Only then should the family decide how much they’re willing to invest.

College Rankings Are Finally Moving Toward the Question Families Should Have Been Asking

The most interesting thing about the new U.S. News ranking isn’t that MIT replaced Princeton. It’s why the ranking changed. One of America’s most influential college-ranking systems is placing greater emphasis on what happens after graduation. That’s an important shift.

For families, the objective shouldn’t be getting into the highest-ranked college possible. It should be helping a student identify a meaningful career direction and finding an educational pathway that prepares them for it, at a cost that makes sense for their family situation.

That’s the difference between simply paying for college and getting a return on the education.
