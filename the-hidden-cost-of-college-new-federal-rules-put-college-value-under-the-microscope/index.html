The Hidden Cost of College: New Federal Rules Put College Value Under the Microscope

November 7, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Table of Contents

  2. 2. A Four-Year Degree That Takes Five Years Changes the Entire Investment
  3. 3. Federal Regulators Are Putting Greater Attention on Student Outcomes

When families calculate the cost of college, they typically focus on three numbers: tuition, scholarships and the amount they expect to pay out of pocket. But what if some of the biggest college expenses aren’t included in those calculations?

What happens when a student changes colleges and loses a semester of credits? What if a degree takes five years instead of four? Or what if a student graduates with significant debt but limited earning potential?

These are real financial risks, and new federal regulations are bringing greater attention to them. On October 30, 2026, the U.S. Department of Education published a final rule designed to modernize college accreditation, strengthen certain student protections and place greater emphasis on educational outcomes. The regulations are scheduled to take effect July 1, 2027.

For financial advisors working with families preparing for college, the message is clear: The cost of college isn’t simply what a university charges. It’s what a family ultimately spends to help a student achieve a meaningful career outcome.

1. Lost College Credits Can Become a Major Financial Expense

One of the most significant provisions in the new regulations addresses the transfer of college credits. Historically, students transferring between colleges have sometimes discovered that courses completed at one institution do not count toward their degree at another.

According to a U.S. Government Accountability Office study cited by the Department of Education, students who transferred colleges lost an average of approximately 43% of their previously earned credits.

Think about what that could mean financially. Imagine a student attending a university with an annual net cost of $40,000. After two years, the student decides to transfer to another institution because the original college no longer fits their academic or career goals. If the student loses enough credits to require one additional semester, the family could face approximately $20,000 in additional college costs. An extra full academic year could cost $40,000. And those figures don’t account for the financial impact of delaying entry into the workforce.

A college transfer can become a five-figure financial decision, even when the new school’s tuition is lower.

What the New Rules Change

Under the final regulations, accrediting agencies must establish stronger standards for how institutions evaluate transfer credits. The provisions include requirements addressing comparable coursework, written explanations for certain credit denials and appeals processes. Institutions must also provide information about the timing of transfer-credit evaluations so prospective transfer students can understand which credits will be accepted before making certain financial commitments.

The regulations do not guarantee that every course will transfer or count toward a student’s chosen major. But they are intended to make the process more consistent and transparent. For families, the lesson is simple: before choosing a college, investigate what happens if the student changes direction.

2. A Four-Year Degree That Takes Five Years Changes the Entire Investment

Families often approach college planning with a four-year financial model. But what happens when the student needs additional time?

Student B’s education costs $35,000 more, despite attending a college with the same annual price. If that student also delays entering a career with a starting salary of $60,000, the broader financial opportunity cost could be substantial.

This is why families should look beyond a college’s published tuition and examine graduation rates, degree requirements and access to required courses. A college offering a generous scholarship may not be the best financial choice if students routinely struggle to complete their programs on time.

3. Federal Regulators Are Putting Greater Attention on Student Outcomes

The new accreditation regulations also strengthen the role of program-level student outcomes in evaluating educational quality. The federal government is placing greater emphasis on measures that help assess whether educational programs are delivering meaningful results – including student completion, employment-related outcomes and other measures appropriate to particular programs.

For financial advisors, this raises an important question: Are families evaluating colleges based on what they hope the institution will provide, or on evidence of what students actually achieve?

4. College Affordability Is About More Than Reducing Tuition

Another objective of the federal accreditation reforms is to reduce unnecessary barriers and costs in higher education. For example, consider two colleges:

  • College A publishes tuition of $65,000 but offers the student $35,000 in renewable institutional scholarships. Annual remaining cost: $30,000.
  • College B publishes tuition of $38,000 but offers only $5,000 in financial assistance. Annual remaining cost: $33,000.

The more expensive college is actually $3,000 less expensive based on this simplified tuition comparison. This is why financial advisors should encourage families to evaluate total four-year net cost, not published tuition or scholarship size alone.

5. Where Is Your Student Wanted?

At Pathfinders College & Career Advisors, one of the most important questions we encourage families to consider is: Where is your student wanted? A thoughtful college list should account for:

  • Career alignment and the strength of the intended academic program.
  • Net cost after scholarships and financial assistance.
  • Graduation and retention outcomes.
  • Transfer-credit policies and flexibility if interests change.
  • Internship opportunities, employment preparation and likely earnings.

Six Questions For Families

  1. Does your student have a clear career direction? Career exploration can help narrow the list of appropriate majors, colleges and educational pathways.
  2. What is the family’s total education budget? This should include 529 savings, anticipated cash flow, potential borrowing and any graduate education that may be required.
  3. What will the college actually cost after financial aid? Evaluate the complete net cost, including housing and other expenses, rather than relying on published tuition or scholarship announcements.
  4. What percentage of students graduate in four years? Graduation timelines can materially change the total investment.
  5. What happens if the student changes majors or transfers? Families should understand credit-transfer policies, major requirements and the potential cost of changing direction.
  6. What are the career outcomes for graduates in the student’s intended field? Look for relevant information about internships, graduate placement, employment and earnings.

The Biggest College Financial Mistake May Happen Before the First Tuition Payment

The new federal accreditation rules are an important reminder that college value should be measured through more than tuition and financial aid. Families need to understand the relationship between educational choices, time to completion, career outcomes and long-term financial well-being. The smartest college investment begins before the college list is created. It begins with understanding the student.
