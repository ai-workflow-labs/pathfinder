Private Student Lending Nearly Doubles at SoFi. What Does That Mean for Families Paying for College?

November 3, 2026 · By Susan Cornish

Table of Contents

  1. Private Student Lending Is Growing Faster Than Expected
  2. Why Federal Loan Limits Haven’t Kept Pace With College Costs
  3. The Hidden Risk of Private Student Lending
  4. What This Means for Families Paying for College
  5. The Better Approach: Plan Before the Borrowing Begins

When families think about paying for college, the conversation usually starts with savings, scholarships, and federal student loans. Private student loans tend to come later in the process, often treated as a last resort after other options are exhausted.

But recent data from SoFi Technologies suggests that families may be turning to private lending earlier – and in greater numbers. For financial advisors working with families who have college-bound students, this trend is worth watching closely.

Private Student Lending Is Growing Faster Than Expected

SoFi Technologies reported that its student loan originations nearly doubled in the third quarter of 2026 compared to the same period in 2025. The company attributed this growth in part to continuing confusion around federal student loan servicing, borrowers looking for more competitive interest rates, and families seeking financing options beyond the federal loan system.

This isn’t simply a story about one lender’s quarterly results. It reflects a broader shift in how families are approaching college financing at a time when college costs continue to rise and federal loan limits remain largely unchanged.

Why Federal Loan Limits Haven’t Kept Pace With College Costs

The federal Direct Loan program caps the amount undergraduate students can borrow each year. For dependent undergraduates, annual limits range from $5,500 for first-year students to $7,500 for third- and fourth-year students. The aggregate limit for dependent undergraduates is $31,000.

These limits have remained largely unchanged for years. Meanwhile, the total annual cost of attending a four-year college – including tuition, housing, meals, fees, books, and other expenses – can easily exceed $50,000, $60,000, or $70,000 at many institutions.

The gap between what federal loans will cover and what college actually costs has pushed many families toward alternative financing options. For some, that means Parent PLUS Loans. For others, it means private student loans – with potentially significant long-term consequences.

The Hidden Risk of Private Student Lending

Private student loans are fundamentally different from federal loans in several important ways. Federal loans offer income-driven repayment options, deferment and forbearance protections, and in some cases, loan forgiveness programs. Private loans typically offer fewer repayment options and less flexibility during financial hardship.

Interest rates on private student loans can be variable, meaning payments could increase if rates rise. Creditworthiness affects the terms a borrower receives, which can be a disadvantage for students with limited credit history. And unlike federal loans, private loans generally require a creditworthy co-signer – often a parent – which can create liability that persists for years or decades.

When families turn to private lending to fill a financing gap, they may be solving a short-term problem while creating a long-term burden – for themselves or for their student.

What This Means for Families Paying for College

The growth in private student lending is a signal worth paying attention to. It suggests that many families are reaching the limits of what federal loans, savings, and institutional aid can cover – and are filling the gap with private debt.

For financial advisors working with these families, this creates a conversation opportunity. How much total debt is the family taking on? What are the repayment terms? How does the borrowing amount compare to the student’s expected earning potential in their chosen career field?

These are questions that families often don’t think to ask until after they’ve already committed to a college and a financing plan. By then, the options for reducing the debt burden are limited.

The Better Approach: Plan Before the Borrowing Begins

The families most likely to avoid excessive student loan debt are those who plan strategically before choosing a college – not after receiving the acceptance letters.

That planning starts with understanding what the family can realistically afford to spend on college. It continues with building a college list that balances academic and career fit with financial value. And it requires evaluating the full financial aid package – not just the scholarship headline – to understand what the student and family will actually pay.

At Pathfinders College & Career Advisors, we work with students and families to approach college as an investment decision as well as an educational one. That means starting with career exploration – helping students identify what they want to do after college – and working backward to identify the academic programs, colleges, and financial pathways most likely to support those goals.

When families understand the relationship between college choice, career direction, and financing, they are better positioned to make decisions they can sustain. Schedule a strategy call with Pathfinders today and start building a college plan that works for your family’s financial future.
